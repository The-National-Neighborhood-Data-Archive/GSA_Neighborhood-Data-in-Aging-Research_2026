Slide 3: Can the neighborhood you live in change the way your immune system ages?

Slide 4: Why look at the immune system?

  • Our immune system ages
  • An “older” immune system is less able to fight off new infections and is associated with many chronic conditions
  • Chronic stress and repeated infections may speed that aging up
  • Neighborhoods may be a key source of stress and/or infectious diseases

Slide 5: Three signs of immune aging

1

2

3

CD4+ “helper” T cells

CD8+ “killer” T cells

CMV antibodies

The balance of worn, experienced cells to fresh, ready-for-anything (naïve) cells. Measured as a ratio of older: younger cells.

The same balance: more experienced cells relative to naïve cells means an older profile. Measured as a ratio of older: younger cells.

CMV is a common virus that stays in the body for life. Higher antibody levels suggest the immune system is working harder to keep it in check.

For all three, higher values point to an “older” immune profile.

Slide 6: The data: Health and Retirement Study

  • Nationally representative study of Americans over 50
  • Running since 1992, with follow-up every two years
  • More than 20,000 participants
  • In 2016, blood samples measured immune function
  • We also knew where people lived in 2016

Slide 9: What we pulled from NaNDA

Neighborhood disadvantage

  • % living below the poverty line
  • % unemployed
  • % receiving public assistance or food stamps
  • % female-headed households with children

Neighborhood affluence

  • % households earning over $75K
  • % in professional or managerial jobs
  • % with a bachelor’s degree or higher

Also: population density

How crowded the neighborhood is

Slide 10: What we found: CMV antibodies

Image: Forest plot in two panels showing how CMV antibody levels differ by neighborhood, across five models that add controls one step at a time: 1, age and gender; 2, plus income and education; 3, plus race/ethnicity; 4, plus health behaviors and conditions; 5, plus population density. Left panel, more neighborhood disadvantage: all five estimates sit to the right of the no-difference line, meaning higher antibodies. The estimate is largest in model 1, shrinks in models 2 and 3, then holds steady through model 5. Right panel, more neighborhood affluence: all five estimates sit to the left of the line, meaning lower antibodies, and move closer to zero as controls are added. All ten confidence intervals exclude zero.

More disadvantage → higher CMV antibodies. More affluence → lower.

The link shrinks once we account for people’s own circumstances, but it never goes away.

Slide 13: Why NaNDA made this possible

  • Ready-made neighborhood measures for the whole U.S.
  • Links to any study with a geographic ID, like a census tract
  • Consistent definitions, so results can be compared across studies
  • Lets health researchers examine whether where you live shows up in how you age

Slide 19: Backup: Who was in the study (N = 9,933)

Demographics

  • Median age 67; 59% female
  • 64% NH White, 18% NH Black, 15% Hispanic, 3% other
  • Education: 17% less than HS, 51% HS grad, 7% some college, 24% college grad

Neighborhood, median (IQR)

  • Disadvantage: 7.81 (7.5)
  • Affluence: 28.37 (20.89)

Immune measures, median (IQR)

  • CD8+:CD4+ ratio: 0.33 (0.31)
  • CD4+ EMRA:naïve: 0.04 (0.12)
  • CD8+ EMRA:naïve: 2.31 (4.88)
  • CMV: 72% seropositive; IgG 214.2 U/mL (578.25)
